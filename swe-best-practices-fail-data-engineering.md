## Notes

- **Slide 1 — opening hook:** *Pipeline fails. The instinct: blame the last deploy.*
  - The SWE reflex — when prod breaks, look at what shipped most recently. In app-land this is usually right: code is the only thing that changed, so `git bisect`/last-deploy-rollback is a high-hit-rate first move.
  - In data engineering this instinct **misleads more often than it helps**. Pipelines fail for reasons that have nothing to do with your code: upstream schema drift, a partner sending bad data, a late-arriving partition, a quota hit, a silent type coercion in a warehouse, DST/timezone weirdness, a row that finally exceeded a limit after six months of growth. The code didn't change — *the world did*.
  - Sets up the talk's core thesis: SWE best practices assume code is the variable. In data, **data is the variable** — and practices that don't account for that fail or actively mislead.
  - **Speaker's framing line:** *"the data engineers know it's probably not the last deploy."*
    - Tells you who's in the room and who isn't. **Data engineers have already internalized this** — they don't reach for the deploy log first; they reach for the source data, the upstream job, the partition stats. The "blame the last deploy" instinct is a *SWE-side* reflex that data folks have already trained out of themselves.
    - The talk isn't aimed at data engineers — it's aimed at the **SWE-cultured stakeholders around them** (platform teams, eng managers, infra folks importing app-eng playbooks) who keep prescribing SWE practices that don't load-bear in a data context. The data engineers in the audience are nodding; the SWE-leaning ones are the target.
  - **Speaker's contrast:** *"in application dev, the instinct is right."*
    - Explicit concession — she's not saying "blame the last deploy" is wrong in general. In app dev it usually **is** the last deploy: code is the dominant input, deploys are the dominant change-event, and the rollback button is well-defined. The SWE reflex was learned in an environment where it pays off.
    - The point is the **transfer failure**: a practice that's correct in app dev gets imported into data eng, where the dominant input isn't code, and the same reflex stops paying off. The talk's pattern is going to be: *here's a SWE practice that earns its place in app dev; here's why the same practice misfires when applied to pipelines.*
  - **Speaker's aphorism:** *"if you control what ships, you control what breaks."*
    - Crisp statement of why SWE practices work in app dev: the change surface is **closed**. Deploys are gated, code is the only input that varies, rollback is a button. The whole SWE toolkit (CI, code review, canaries, feature flags, blue/green) is built around that one assumption — control the shipment, control the failure mode.
    - Implied setup for the next beat: **in data engineering you don't control what ships.** Upstream producers ship schema changes you didn't approve, vendors ship malformed CSVs, a salesperson ships a new region into the CRM, an LLM ships a new output format. Your pipeline is downstream of dozens of "deploys" that aren't yours. The premise that makes the SWE toolkit work — *closed change surface* — doesn't hold.
    - Good quote to lift verbatim; it's the talk's pivot line between "why SWE practices work where they work" and "why they don't transfer."
  - **Data eng tried to import this** — concrete examples on screen: **Airflow** (treating DAGs as deployable code artifacts, versioned in git) and **CI/CD pipelines for data pipelines** (PR review, tests, staged deploys for ETL code).
    - Honest framing — she's not saying data engineers refused the SWE playbook. They **adopted it**: code-as-DAGs, infra-as-code, PR-based review, staged environments. The whole Astronomer/dbt-cloud commercial layer exists *because* data eng absorbed CI/CD patterns.
    - **Speaker's setup line:** *"the deploy is 'load-bearing' till…"* — quote marks around "load-bearing" suggest she's flagging this as a deliberate framing. The deploy-centric SWE model **worked** in data eng for a while — *load-bearing*, i.e., the thing the rest of your reliability story rests on — **until** some breaking-point condition that's about to land in the next beat.

- **Concrete example on slide:** an **Airflow ETL DAG** being used as the running example.
  - Sensible choice — Airflow is the data-eng audience's lingua franca, and ETL is the canonical pipeline shape (extract from source → transform → load to warehouse). Keeps the discussion grounded rather than abstract.

- **Headline finding from the 400M-run study — a stark power law:**
  - **3% of DAGs cause 67% of failures.**
  - **60% of DAGs almost never fail.**
  - Classic Pareto/long-tail distribution — failure is not uniformly spread across pipelines; it's concentrated in a tiny minority. The middle ~37% are the "occasional failers"; the bulk of operational pain comes from a small set of repeat offenders.
  - **This reframes the entire reliability problem.** If you treat every DAG with the same SWE rigor (universal CI, universal tests, universal review gates), you're spending most of your budget on the 60% that wasn't going to fail anyway, while the 3% that's burning down prod gets the same baseline treatment as a daily report.
  - Directly undermines the "uniform best practices" SWE instinct — in app dev you harden every service because any of them can be the next outage; in data eng the failure distribution tells you **most of your effort should be aimed at a known-bad 3%**.
  - Strongly implied next move: instead of "apply SWE practices everywhere," the lesson is **identify the 3% and treat them differently** (more guardrails, more observability, more SLA discipline) — and stop wasting cycles uniformly hardening the 60% tail of stable DAGs.
  - Open question to watch for: **what makes the 3% the 3%?** Is it a property of the DAG (complexity, # of upstream sources, # of operators), of the data (volume, schema volatility, vendor source), or of the team (ownership, on-call coverage)? Whichever lever she identifies is the actionable takeaway.
- **Where the failures actually live — three concentrations from the data:**
  - **1. ~Half (1-in-2) of failures concentrate on ONE specific runtime version.**
    - **Correction from my live notes**: the slide's exact framing is *"~½ OF FAILURES concentrated on one runtime version"* — not "buggy versions" plural. It's a single bad Airflow runtime version sitting under a huge fraction of the failure population.
    - That sharpens the diagnosis considerably: there's a **specific known-bad runtime out there** that everyone keeps either running on or upgrading to, and it dominates the failure stats. The remediation isn't "be more careful about runtime versions in general" — it's "stop running this one."
    - This is a *huge* signal. The SWE reflex says "review user code carefully" — but if half your failures are coming from the runtime, no amount of DAG-level PR review will catch them. The remediation is upstream: runtime version pinning discipline, staged rollouts of platform versions, regression-testing the runtime itself before promoting it.
    - Also undercuts "blame the last deploy" *of the DAG* — the deploy that broke things often wasn't the user's, it was the platform's. Confirms the opening thesis from a different angle.
  - **2. Long-running workflows fail at 3–5× the rate of typical ones.**
    - Duration is itself a failure-rate multiplier. Intuitive but the magnitude is striking — a workflow that runs for hours has 3–5× the failure exposure of a short one, presumably from compounding upstream-flakiness, transient infra errors, resource pressure, timeout windows expiring, and "more time = more surface area for the world to change underneath you."
    - Implication: **duration is a risk axis SWE practices don't natively model.** App-dev requests are short; you don't think about a request "running long enough to get unlucky." Pipelines do. Mitigation here is structural — break long workflows into checkpoint-able sub-DAGs, add retry-with-resume, push state into durable storage so a failure at hour 4 doesn't redo hours 1–3.
  - **3. Some DAGs are running at a 97% failure rate — i.e., "chronically broken."**
    - This one is wild and worth lingering on. A 97% failure rate means **the pipeline essentially never succeeds**, and yet *it's still scheduled, still running, still producing alerts*. Somebody is tolerating this — either nobody owns it, alerts are muted, or the downstream consumer has given up and stopped caring.
    - Strong evidence for a category SWE practices have no name for: **zombie pipelines** — code that's "in production" by every technical definition (scheduled, deployed, monitored) but is socially dead (no one fixes it, no one consumes the output, the failures are background noise).
    - In app-dev terms this would be a service returning 500s on 97% of requests and nobody paging — unthinkable. In data eng it's *common enough to show up at population scale across 400M runs*. That contrast is itself the talk's argument: the operational norms differ because the failure semantics differ (a missing daily report is not a missing API response).
    - Action implication: **alert hygiene & ownership audits** are higher-leverage than more tests/CI/review. Find the 97%-failure DAGs, identify whether anyone consumes them, and either fix them or retire them. SWE practices for the *living* code aren't going to save you from the dead code that's still running.
  - **The bigger pattern across all three:** none of these failure concentrations are addressable by classic SWE hygiene applied to the DAG. They're about **runtime/platform versions, workflow duration, and pipeline-ownership rot** — categories that SWE practices either don't model or model poorly. This is the empirical case for the talk's title.
  - **Speaker's punchline on the three findings:** *"none of these trace back to a deploy."*
    - This is the moment the opening hook lands. The whole talk opened with "pipeline fails → instinct is blame the last deploy" — and now the empirical bill comes due. Across 400M runs, the **three biggest failure concentrations are all non-deploy phenomena**: buggy runtime versions (platform's deploy, not yours), long-running workflows (duration accumulating exposure to a changing world), chronically broken DAGs (social/ownership decay, not a code event).
    - Devastating to the SWE-imported playbook. If your reliability posture is "ship carefully, gate deploys, rollback fast" — you've optimized for a failure mode that **isn't where the failures are**. The deploy review process can be perfect and you'd still own the 3%/67% problem.
    - This is the "load-bearing till" cliff-hanger from earlier resolving: the deploy *was* load-bearing in app dev's failure model, and SWE imported that model into data eng — but when you actually measure where data-eng failures live, **the deploy isn't holding the weight**. It was load-bearing in theory, not in the data.
    - Best single line to lift verbatim from this section — it's the empirical kill-shot on the opening reflex.
  - **Speaker's follow-through:** *"failures [are] not associated with deploys — it's just where our attention goes."*
    - Pivots the argument from *empirical* ("the deploy isn't where the failure lives") to *psychological* ("but we keep looking there anyway"). This is a sharper claim — she's saying the SWE reflex isn't just imprecise, it's an **attention bug**. We have a 400M-run dataset telling us failures concentrate in runtime versions, long workflows, and zombie DAGs — and we still triage by opening the deploy log.
    - The cost isn't just "we miss the real cause" — it's **opportunity cost on attention**. Every incident where the on-call instinctively chases the last deploy is one where the runtime regression, the duration-driven flake, or the 97%-failure zombie isn't getting investigated. The misallocation is systematic, not incidental.
    - This is also where the talk's audience-targeting lands. The data engineers in the room already know failures aren't deploy-driven (per the earlier "the data engineers know" line). The point of saying it now is to name the **cognitive habit** so SWE-leaning teammates can recognize themselves in it — "you keep looking at deploys because that's where you were trained to look, not because that's where the bugs are."
    - Connects to a broader pattern in incident response: **availability bias** in triage. You search where the streetlight is — i.e., the systems you have good visibility into (deploy logs, CI history, git blame) — even when the actual fault lives in systems you don't (vendor data, runtime image build, scheduler internals). Data-eng failure modes mostly live in the dark.
- **The naïve SWE prescription — and why it breaks:** the standard advice when you hear "buggy runtime version is half your failures" is *"just pick a version and adjust the DAG code"* or *"just containerize the DAGs."* Speaker's point: **neither is actually easy.**
  - **Concrete failure case she walks through:**
    - **DAG 1** — vendor API integration. Pins `requests` to a specific version (because the vendor SDK requires that version, or a known bug above that version exists).
    - **DAG 2** — ML model workflow. Needs a **different** version of `requests` (likely because some ML library — `transformers`, `boto3`, an inference client — pulled in a conflicting constraint).
    - Both DAGs live in the **same Airflow environment**, sharing one Python runtime. You can't satisfy both pins simultaneously with a single virtualenv.
  - This is **dependency-hell at the platform level**, not just at the project level. In app dev, each service is its own deployable with its own pinned environment, so dependency conflicts stay local. In Airflow, a single worker runs dozens-to-hundreds of DAGs in the same Python process tree by default — and they have to share `requests`, `boto3`, `pandas`, every transitive dep, with each other.
  - **Why "just pick a version" doesn't work:** there's no "right" version. Either the vendor DAG breaks or the ML DAG breaks. The SWE habit of "resolve to a single coherent dependency tree" *assumes there is one*; in shared-runtime data eng, that assumption fails.
  - **Why "just containerize" isn't easy either:** containerizing per-DAG is the obviously-correct answer in theory (give each DAG its own image), but it's operationally expensive — image build pipelines per DAG, image registry churn, per-task container cold-start latency, more infra to schedule, more for the platform team to maintain. Airflow has `KubernetesPodOperator` / `DockerOperator` for exactly this, and Astronomer ships per-DAG environment isolation, but **adopting it is non-trivial** — it changes how teams ship, debug, and observe their DAGs.
  - **The deeper point:** the SWE-style recommendation ("isolate dependencies per service") is *correct*, but it's not free. Importing it into data eng means absorbing real operational cost — and that cost is why most teams *don't* do it, which is why the buggy-runtime failure mode keeps showing up at population scale. **The advice isn't wrong; the friction of applying it is the whole problem.**
  - **Speaker's framing line:** *"the engineer recommendation isn't easy."* — explicit acknowledgment that this isn't a matter of data engineers being unaware of best practices. They know what the SWE-textbook answer is. The answer just doesn't cleanly apply to the shape of their work.

- **The structural diagram — why the two disciplines are different at the system level:**
  - **App dev:** `code → input & behavior → outputs`. **Code drives outputs.** The dominant causal arrow runs from your code to what users see. Inputs (HTTP requests, user actions) vary, but code is the thing *you* write and *you* control — so reliability is mostly a function of code quality. The SWE toolkit (tests, CI, review, deploys, rollbacks) is shaped around this arrow: protect the code path, and you protect the outputs.
  - **Data eng:** `upstream data + code structure + dependencies → outputs`. **Data shapes the code.** Three roughly co-equal inputs feed the output, and **upstream data is one of them** — not a parameter your code processes, but a *first-class input that determines what your code even has to look like*. If the vendor sends a new column, the schema of your code changes. If the data volume 10×s, your code's structure (chunking, parallelism, memory model) changes. If a new data source comes online, the pipeline topology changes.
  - **The asymmetry that breaks SWE practice import:**
    - In app dev, code is the **independent variable**; inputs/outputs are **dependent** on it. You optimize for code quality and inputs/outputs follow.
    - In data eng, **data is co-causal with code** — and arguably the *more* independent variable, because you can't control what upstream produces. Code is partly *dependent on data*. You optimize for code quality and you still get failures, because the other inputs (upstream data, dep tree) shifted underneath you.
  - **"Data shapes the code"** is the load-bearing phrase. It's not just that data flows through code — data **determines the code's shape**. Schema changes force code changes. Volume changes force architecture changes. New sources force DAG topology changes. The code is *downstream of the data's reality*, which is the inverse of the app-dev mental model where the code is upstream of the user's experience.
  - This is the **structural explanation** for every empirical finding so far:
    - *Why aren't failures deploy-driven?* → Because deploys only change one of three inputs (code). The other two (upstream data, deps) change continuously without any deploy event at all.
    - *Why are runtime versions half the failures?* → Because deps are a first-class input on the same level as code, but managed by a different team on a different cadence.
    - *Why do long-running workflows fail more?* → Because longer windows = more time for the upstream-data input to drift while your job is running.
    - *Why are there 97%-failure zombies?* → Because the data input changed permanently (vendor deprecated an endpoint, schema evolved) and no one updated the code-shape to match — but the schedule kept running.
  - **Implication for what "best practices" should even *mean* in data eng:** practices need to be shaped around the **three-input model**, not the one-input model. That probably means: data contracts + schema validation at boundaries (govern the upstream-data input), runtime/dep promotion discipline (govern the dependency input), and code review (govern the code input) — *as three separate disciplines*, not all collapsed into "do CI/CD better."
  - Worth sketching the two diagrams side-by-side in your own notes — this is the conceptual armature the rest of the talk is going to hang on.
- **The killshot scenario — SWE-correct, data-broken:** *"your tests pass, your CI/CD is green. Then upstream drops a column."*
  - Concrete instantiation of the three-input model. Every SWE-shaped signal says "shipped, healthy, working": tests green, CI green, deploy clean. **Nothing in the code changed and nothing in the code is wrong.** And yet at the next scheduled run, the pipeline blows up — because the upstream-data input mutated outside the code-input's control loop.
  - This is what makes the "blame the last deploy" reflex so misleading: there *was no last deploy*. The most recent change wasn't yours, it was the data producer's. Your `git log` is clean; their schema migration is the actual deploy that broke prod, and you don't have visibility into it.
  - **Highlights the asymmetric remediation:** classic SWE remediation surface here is *useless*.
    - You can't `git revert` an upstream column drop.
    - Your tests pass because they tested *your* code against your fixtures, not the data the world is actually sending you today.
    - Your CI gate fired green because CI gates code, not data.
    - Your monitoring saw the deploy succeed, because the deploy did succeed — the *job* succeeded; only the *output* is wrong.
  - **The class of failure has no analog in app dev.** The closest equivalent would be "your service is up, returning 200s, but the database underneath you silently dropped a column and now the JSON is malformed." App-dev architectures actively prevent this (schema migrations are coordinated, the DB is *yours*). Data eng is structurally exposed to it because the upstream isn't yours.
  - **What actually catches this** (foreshadowing remediations she'll likely propose): schema validation at the ingest boundary, data contracts with upstream producers, anomaly detection on output rows/columns, freshness/shape SLOs that fire independently of the job-success signal. **None of these are SWE practices** — they're data-platform practices that exist because the SWE practices don't cover this surface.
  - One of the talk's best one-liners — short, vivid, and any data engineer in the audience has lived this exact incident. Lift verbatim.
- **The recovery-cohort finding — code change as a diagnostic signal:**
  - From the 400M-run dataset, she partitioned DAGs into cohorts by health trajectory and looked at how much code change accompanied each. **DAGs that went from broken → healthy ("the recovery cohort") had ~4× the code-change volume** of the cohort that flipped the other way.
  - **CORRECTION from my live notes** — slide shows the 1/2/4 ratios are *(309 flipped healthy→broken : Other degraded DAGs : Recovery broken→healthy) = 1× : 2× : 4×*. My original gloss had this backwards — the "1" is NOT a stable baseline, it's **the cohort that fell apart**. Critical distinction: DAGs that *broke* are getting **the fewest** code changes, DAGs that *recovered* are getting **the most**. Code change correlates with *repair*, not *breakage*.
  - The headline reframe — **she's flipping the deploy/code lens from villain to instrument:**
    - The opening hook said "blame the last deploy" is the wrong reflex because failures aren't deploy-shaped.
    - This finding says: **but the *presence* of code change is still informative — just in the opposite direction from the SWE instinct.** When code change *accompanies* a transition from broken → healthy, the failure really was code-shaped and code-fixable. When a DAG goes broken → healthy *without* code change, the cause was non-code (upstream data resumed, vendor schema reverted, runtime version got pinned at the platform layer, transient infra cleared).
  - **The diagnostic rule she's distilling:** *"if code change, it's the code. If not, it's the inputs / data pipeline."*
    - Beautifully crisp. Code change presence/absence at the moment of recovery is a high-signal classifier of root-cause class. It doesn't tell you *what* went wrong, but it tells you *which family of cause* — and that's enough to send the on-call to the right place (deploy log vs. upstream contact vs. runtime team).
    - This is a **counter-intuitive use of `git log`**: not as the first place to look for blame ("which commit broke it?"), but as a *post-hoc classifier* of *which kind of failure you just had*. Same data, opposite framing.
  - **Speaker's framing line:** *"the absence of code change [as a recovery signal] is [itself] a signal."*
    - Classic Sherlock-Holmes-curious-incident-of-the-dog framing: the *null observation* carries information. In SWE incident response we mostly look at what changed; she's pointing out that "nothing in the repo changed and it healed anyway" is one of the most diagnostic possible observations — it definitively rules out code as cause.
    - Practically: incident retros should ask not just "what code changed?" but **"did anything in our code need to change for this to resolve?"** A no-answer there pushes the next investigation upward to the data/runtime/platform layer.
  - **This also redeems the SWE practices that earlier beats appeared to demolish.** Her argument isn't "throw out CI/CD and code review" — it's that **those practices govern one of three inputs**, and the 4×-code-change recovery cohort is exactly the population where SWE practices actually *do* pay off, because for those DAGs the cause was code-shaped. The talk's nuance is: **don't abandon SWE practices, abandon the assumption that they cover everything.**
  - **For your own pushback queue (re: data versioning question):** the 1/2/4 cohort framing implicitly uses code change as a proxy because code is the easily-observable input. A more complete classifier would partition the same cohorts by *data-version change* and *runtime-version change* alongside code change — three diagnostic axes, one per input in her three-input model. If she doesn't extend the analysis that way, it's a good Q&A prompt: *"Does your study have visibility into runtime-version churn or upstream-data version churn the way it does for code churn? Could you build an equivalent recovery-cohort signal for those two inputs?"*

  - **Move 1 — Contract-test your inputs, not just your code.** *(→ Layer 2: data correctness.)*
    - The reframe: shift from "we test that our code is correct" (SWE habit) to "**we test that the input we received is what we agreed to receive.**" The verification surface moves from the code artifact outward to the ingest boundary.

She's saying: data eng needs the same idea, applied to **upstream data producers**.   - **Move 2 — Treat time AND date states as first-class concerns.** *(→ Layer 3: time correctness.)*
    - **Refinement on the verbatim slide:** she's explicitly pairing **time** (continuous temporal moments — timestamps, freshness, latencies, durations) with **date states** (discrete temporal artifacts — "the partition for 2026-05-15," "the daily batch for last Tuesday," "the as-of snapshot for end-of-quarter"). These are *two different first-class concerns* that data eng routinely conflates.
    - Time is *continuous and instantaneous* — "is this data < 1 hour stale?", "did this job complete within 2 minutes?", "what's the event-time vs processing-time skew?"
    - Date state is *discrete and named* — "the 2026-05-15 partition is complete," "the end-of-quarter snapshot is frozen," "the Q1 retrospective table is immutable as of close." A date state is a **logical artifact** with identity, lifecycle, and semantics — not just a clock reading.
    - Why this distinction matters: most data-eng bugs at layer 3 come from **silently coercing one into the other**. Treating "today's partition" as a function of `now()` (continuous time) when it should be a named state ("the 2026-05-15 partition, regardless of when this code runs") is a classic source of late-arriving-data bugs, replay non-idempotency, and backfill weirdness. The partition for 2026-05-15 should mean the same thing whether you process it at 3am on the 16th, replay it next week, or backfill it next year.
    - The right framing **forces you to name your date states explicitly** and write code that's parametric over them, rather than implicit reads of system time. This is why dbt has `{{ var('execution_date') }}`, Airflow has `logical_date` (formerly `execution_date`), and table formats like Iceberg expose explicit time-travel — they're trying to give you a clean handle on date-state-as-data-state, separate from wall-clock time.
    - Direct prescriptive callout of the time layer. The framing "first-class concern" is doing a lot — in app dev, time is *implicit* (every request has a "now," and "now" is unambiguous); in data eng, time is **explicit, multi-valued, and contested** (event-time vs processing-time vs ingestion-time vs partition-time vs SLO-time), and pretending otherwise causes silent correctness failures.
    - Practical instrumentation: **freshness SLOs** ("the latest record in this table must be < 1 hour old"), **explicit event-time vs processing-time discipline** (Beam/Flink-style watermarks, or at minimum, store both timestamps), **partition health monitoring** (every expected partition arrived; none silently empty), **idempotent re-runnable jobs** so backfills produce the same output regardless of when they execute, and crucially **time-travel-capable storage** (Iceberg/Delta) so a pipeline can ask "what did this table look like *as of yesterday at 3pm*?" — important for both reproducibility and root-cause investigation.
    - **This is where data versioning is finally load-bearing in her talk.** The earlier gap I flagged on your behalf (re: Iceberg/lakeFS/DVC) closes here — time-as-first-class is the layer where versioned tables earn their keep.     - Why this lands second: layer 2 (input contracts) gives you *static* correctness on each ingest event; layer 3 (time) gives you *temporal* correctness across ingest events. You need both, and contracts have to come first because without a contract you don't know what "the data" even *is* to reason about its freshness.
  - **Move 3 — Design for the input you'll *get*, not just the input you *expect*.** *(→ spans Layers 2 & 4-5: graceful handling of unexpected data shapes.)*
    - **Verbatim framing from the slide is much sharper than my earlier "input surprise" paraphrase.** The clean contrast is **expect vs. get**: the input you *expect* is the spec — clean schema, on-time, in-range, the contract upstream signed. The input you *get* is reality — late, malformed, duplicated, with bonus columns, missing required fields, encoded in the wrong charset, sometimes just empty. Real production pipelines have to handle the *get* case, not just the *expect* case.
    - This phrasing also subtly indicts the SWE testing model: **unit tests verify behavior against the input you expect** (because you wrote the fixtures). They give you false confidence about the *get* case. A pipeline that's exhaustively tested against expected inputs and untested against realistic-bad inputs is *not* robust — it's *specifically fragile to the failures that will actually happen in prod.*
    - **The Postel's-Law connection still applies** ("be liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you emit") but her framing is more practical: don't moralize about whether inputs *should* be clean, just **engineer for the inputs you'll observe in production** — which includes everything weird the world has thrown at you historically, and a generous allowance for new weirdness you haven't seen yet.
    - The reframe: contract testing (move 1) tells you *when* the upstream broke its agreement. **Designing for input surprise tells you what your pipeline does about it.** Contract violation should not equal "the entire pipeline halts and a human gets paged at 3am" — it should equal "the bad records are dead-lettered, the good records keep flowing, and a clear alert fires that something needs attention."
    - "Input surprise" is a great term — captures that **the producer didn't sign up to honor your contract forever**. Schemas evolve, vendors deprecate, partners send corrupt CSVs, new edge cases appear in long-tail data. The question isn't "can we prevent this?" App dev mostly rejects-and-500s when input is malformed; data eng can't, because *the data is the deliverable* — you have to do something useful with what arrived.

## My questions / pushback / reactions

- **The "just use Coq" reaction.** Reading the five-layer model end-to-end (code correctness, data correctness, time correctness, state-transition safety, recovery & degradation) does feel like an invitation to formal methods — "if you want this many properties verified across an entire pipeline, why not reach for the actual theorem-proving apparatus?" Tempting as the snark is, the honest answer is that the layers she's listing are **specifically the properties that resist formal specification:**
  - *Data correctness* is about agreement with an external producer who hasn't signed your spec and won't.
  - *Time correctness* often hinges on partner SLAs, vendor delivery windows, and out-of-order arrivals that have no closed-form model.
  - *State transitions* could be formally modeled, but they cross trust boundaries (warehouses, lakes, APIs) where no single proof system has jurisdiction.
  - *Recovery and degradation* is partly a socio-technical property (someone has to *care* that the pipeline is broken) — Coq has nothing to say about whether a human will fix your zombie DAG.
  - The reason the four moves are *practices* rather than *proofs* is that the failure surface is **open** (the world keeps adding new failure modes) rather than **closed** (a fixed system you can reason exhaustively about). Formal methods love closed systems; data eng is structurally open. So: snark earned, but the joke also points at a real reason why this discipline reaches for runtime instrumentation (contracts, expectations, SLOs, ownership audits) instead of compile-time proofs.
- **Data versioning gap — resolved.** The talk does land it, just not by name and not centered. Iceberg/Delta-style time-travel is implicit in move 2 (time as first-class), and table-format atomicity is implicit in layer 4 (state transitions). If you wanted a fully complete picture, you'd add an explicit move 5 — *"version your data substrate"* — that names Iceberg/Delta/lakeFS/dbt-snapshots as the technologies. Reasonable Q&A prompt if there's time: *"You implied time-travel storage in moves 2 and 4 but didn't name a substrate — is there a recommendation, or is it tool-agnostic?"*
- **Dynamic-vs-static framing.** Holds up better than I initially gave it credit for. SWE's coverage of layer 1 *is* essentially static analysis of the code artifact (tests, types, linters, review). Layers 2–5 are essentially runtime / observational properties — you can't verify them without watching the system process real inputs. So the analogy maps cleanly: **layer 1 = static-analyzable, layers 2–5 = only knowable dynamically.** Useful mental shorthand, even if it wasn't her explicit framing.

### Refinements on the five-layer model

### Refinements on the four moves

## Q&A

- **Audience tool offerings for the 5 layers** *(an attendee called out specific tools mapped to the model):*
  - **Betamax** — a Python library for **recording and replaying real HTTP interactions** (Python's analog of Ruby's VCR). Cassettes capture actual production responses to disk; tests replay them deterministically.
    - **Maps cleanly to layer 2 (data correctness) — and crucially to move 3 ("design for the input you'll get, not just the input you expect").** Standard unit-test fixtures are *invented* (you write what you *expect* the upstream to send). Betamax cassettes are *captured from prod* (you record what upstream actually sent). That's the difference between testing against your imagination and testing against reality. If your pipeline integrates with a vendor API, replaying real captured payloads catches schema drift, weird edge-case rows, and undocumented field changes that a hand-written fixture would never include.
    - Also has a layer-3 (time correctness) angle — a cassette is implicitly a snapshot of "what the upstream looked like at time T," so you can keep multiple cassettes representing different historical states and test that your pipeline behaves correctly across all of them. Cheap, hand-rolled data versioning for the test environment.
    - Worth noting the limit: Betamax only covers HTTP-shaped upstreams. For database upstreams, file drops, Kafka, etc., you need analog tools (e.g., recorded query results, sample parquet files in a fixture dir, recorded message streams).
  - **Sentry** — error-tracking / exception aggregation platform. Captures unhandled exceptions, deduplicates them, surfaces frequency/regression trends.
    - **Maps to layer 5 (recovery and degradation), with a foot in layer 1.** In a pipeline context Sentry isn't just "where bugs go to be triaged" — it's the substrate that lets you **identify the 3% of DAGs producing 67% of failures** (the recurring exception fingerprints), and the 97%-failure zombies (the exception that's been firing daily for 6 months and nobody acknowledged). It directly enables move 4 ("know your always-broken DAGs") *if* you have ownership/routing rules wired up.
    - Subtle but real layer-2 angle too: many data-correctness failures show up as parser exceptions (couldn't decode that row, schema mismatch, type coercion failed). Aggregated exception views can surface upstream contract violations earlier than dedicated data-quality tooling would.
    - The Sentry comment was a little fragmentary in my notes — [need to confirm what the attendee specifically said about Sentry], but the natural framing is "use exception aggregation as the leading indicator of layer-5 ownership rot."
  - **Broader tool map by layer** *(filling in what *would* round out the audience's suggestions, for completeness — these are the ones commonly named in this space):*
    - Layer 1 — pytest, mypy, ruff/black, dbt's compile-time checks. *(SWE-standard stack.)*
    - Layer 2 — **Great Expectations** (she named this), **dbt tests**, **Soda**, **Monte Carlo**, **Pydantic / Pandera** (typed boundaries), JSON Schema; **Betamax** (replay real captured inputs).
    - Layer 3 — **Apache Iceberg / Delta Lake / Apache Hudi** (table formats with time-travel & snapshots), **lakeFS / Nessie** (catalog versioning), **dbt snapshots** (SCD type 2 in-warehouse), **Airflow's `logical_date`** / Beam/Flink watermarks (explicit event-time semantics).
